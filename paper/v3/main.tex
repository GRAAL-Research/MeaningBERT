\title{Unrelated Is Not Opposed:\\A Signed Scale for Meaning Preservation}

\author{Anonymous Submission}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Meaning preservation metrics output a non-negative score and cannot represent opposition: MeaningBERT scores ``I drink milk'' and ``I do not drink milk'' at $58.30$ out of $100$.
We propose a signed scale on $[-100, 100]$ that weights an existing similarity metric by the probability, given by a polarity head, that one sentence contradicts the other, so unrelated sentences score $0$ and contradictions score below it.
A polarity head trained on inference data with generated identical, unrelated, opposite and antonym pairs catches negations and most unseen antonyms, which a negation word list cannot.
With an unchanged metric, the signed score accepts eleven times fewer contradictions as meaning-preserving at a fixed threshold.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Assessing whether a rewritten sentence preserves the meaning of its source is a prerequisite for evaluating text simplification, summarization and translation \citep{saggion2017automatic}.
The metrics built for this purpose, whether embedding-based (e.g. Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sbert} or BERTScore \citep{zhang2020bertscore}), question-based (e.g. QuestEval \citep{scialom2021questeval}) or trained end to end (e.g. LENS \citep{maddela2023lens} or MeaningBERT \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert}), return one non-negative number, from $100$ for identical meanings to $0$ for unrelated sentences.

This output cannot represent opposition.
Two contradictory sentences are about the same topic and share most of their words, and a metric of shared meaning reads that overlap as preserved meaning.
MeaningBERT scores ``I drink milk'' and ``I do not drink milk'' at $58.30$ out of $100$, in the upper half of the scale.
Following \citet{demarneffe2008contradictions}, we call two sentences contradictory when they are extremely unlikely to be true of the same situation.
This covers explicit negations such as this pair, and also contrary events such as ``a girl is running outside'' and ``a girl is running inside'', which SICK labels as contradictions because both captions describe the same scene.
\citet{baral2026lexflip} observed the same effect on legal text, where edits that reverse the legal force of a sentence barely move embedding metrics.

We therefore propose a signed scale on $[-100, 100]$, on which $100$ denotes identical meanings, $0$ unrelated sentences, and negative values related sentences that assert opposite facts.
We obtain it by composing the score of a similarity metric with a separately trained polarity head.
Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item A meaning preservation metric on a signed scale that distinguishes opposed meaning from unrelated meaning (\autorefpair{Sections}{sec:scale}{sec:composition}).
\item A polarity head, the stratified, leakage-free corpus with derived-polarity data augmentation on which it is trained, and a lexical augmentation that teaches it antonymy (\autorefpair{Sections}{sec:data}{sec:composition}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Meaning Preservation Metrics}

Meaning preservation metrics fall into three families.
Embedding-based metrics compare sentence representations: Sentence-BERT computes the cosine between two sentence embeddings, and BERTScore matches each token to its most similar counterpart in the other sentence.
A negation adds as little as one token, which barely changes either score.
Question-based metrics such as QuestEval generate questions from one text and check whether the other answers them, and a negated sentence, which mentions the same entities, answers most of them.
Learned metrics such as LENS and MeaningBERT replicate human ratings, of overall simplification quality for the former and of meaning preservation for the latter.
These ratings run from identical meaning down to no shared meaning, with no value for opposite meaning, so an annotator can only rate a contradiction low, at the end reserved for unrelated pairs.
The training signal cannot separate the two, and in practice the learned metric does not even score contradictions low, as the milk pair shows.

All these metrics are validated by correlation with human ratings, and MeaningBERT adds two sanity checks, under which identical sentences must score $100$ and unrelated sentences $0$.
None tests opposition: any monotone function of lexical overlap passes both checks \citep{baral2026lexflip}, and ratings that do not distinguish a contradiction from a poor paraphrase cannot reward a metric that does.
Specialized metrics for legal text \citep{beauchemin2025judgebert} and biomedical simplification \citep{lyu2024scigispy} show that general metrics miss small edits with large consequences, but they too output a non-negative score.
We keep such a metric as the similarity component of a signed score and supply the missing direction separately (\autoref{sec:scale}).

\subsection{Natural Language Inference}

Natural language inference provides the polarity that preservation metrics lack.
Large corpora \citep{williams2018mnli,nie2020anli} and fact-verification datasets \citep{thorne2018fever,schuster2021vitaminc} label sentence pairs as entailment, neutral or contradiction, and suites such as MoNLI \citep{geiger2020monli} and NaN-NLI \citep{truong2022nannli} probe negation.
The two tasks are complementary: an inference model says whether the second sentence follows from or denies the first but not how much meaning they share, and a preservation metric measures the shared meaning but not its direction.
We therefore combine them (\autoref{sec:scale}), and SICK \citep{marelli2014sick}, to our knowledge the only resource annotating both over the same pairs, serves to evaluate the combination.
Inference models already serve evaluation: SummaC \citep{laban2022summac} and AlignScore \citep{zha2023alignscore} detect inconsistency between a summary and its source, and MENLI \citep{chen2023menli} combines inference scores with similarity metrics to resist adversarial edits such as negation.
These metrics still output a non-negative score, whereas ours keeps the similarity metric and makes the score signed, so an unrelated pair stays at $0$ and a contradiction falls below it.

\section{A Signed Scale}
\label{sec:scale}

Meaning metrics use an unsigned scale on which $100$ denotes identical meaning and $0$ unrelated sentences, the two anchors of the sanity checks of \citet{beauchemin2023meaningbert}.
We propose a signed scale on $[-100, 100]$.
Its positive half is the unsigned scale with both anchors unchanged.
Its negative half denotes opposite meaning: the sign carries the polarity and the absolute value the closeness of the two sentences, so $-100$ denotes a pair as close as possible in content that asserts the opposite.
We compose the two quantities as
\begin{equation}
s = m \times (1 - 2\, p_{\mathrm{contra}}),
\label{eq:compose}
\end{equation}
with $m \in [0,100]$ the score of any similarity metric and $p_{\mathrm{contra}} \in [0,1]$ the probability of contradiction given by a polarity head.
The polarity head is a classifier that reads the two sentences and, as in natural language inference, outputs a probability for entailment, neutral and contradiction.
Only $p_{\mathrm{contra}}$ enters \autoref{eq:compose}, and \autorefpair{Sections}{sec:data}{sec:setup} describe how we train the head.
The form is a product and not a difference, so an unrelated pair, with $m \approx 0$, scores near zero whatever its polarity, whereas a subtraction would let it drift negative.
\autoref{fig:scale} places five pairs built on ``I drink milk'' on the scale.

One may object that a negation is closer in meaning to its source than an unrelated sentence, so that $100$, $50$ and $0$ would be a defensible order for a paraphrase, a contradiction and an unrelated pair.
This order conflates contradictions with mildly related pairs, since a contradiction and a paraphrase that keeps half of its content both score near $50$.
A signed scale keeps the closeness in $|s|$ and adds the direction.
Human raters confirm that closeness: on the $1$--$5$ relatedness scale of SICK, they rate contradictions $3.59$ on average and neutral pairs $2.98$, in line with the observation that opposites are semantically close \citep{mohammad2013contrast,hill2015simlex}.
Placing contradictions below zero also reflects their cost when a metric decides whether a rewrite may replace its source.
An unrelated output is visible to the reader, but a negation misleads, and factual errors are a central risk of rewriting \citep{maynez2020faithfulness}, to the point that an inaccurate simplification may be worse than none \citep{devaraj2022factuality}.

\input{figure_scale}

The factor $2$ makes the scale symmetric and follows from the definition, not from a fit.
Since $1 - 2\,p_{\mathrm{contra}} = (1 - p_{\mathrm{contra}}) - p_{\mathrm{contra}}$, $m$ is weighted by the margin between the probabilities that the pair does not contradict and that it does.
A certain non-contradiction leaves $m$ unchanged and a certain contradiction maps it to $-m$, so only the sign changes and $s$ stays within $[-100, 100]$ with no clipping.
The sign flips at $p_{\mathrm{contra}} = 0.5$, and a pair the head leans towards contradiction lands in the negative half in proportion to its closeness, as ``I rarely drink milk'' does in \autoref{fig:scale}.
A factor of $1.5$ would truncate the negative half at $-m/2$, and a larger factor would flip the sign on weaker evidence and saturate at $-100$ (\autoref{sec:composition}).
The two ends are anchors: a paraphrase that keeps all the meaning scores $+100$, and the exact negation, identical in content but asserting the opposite, scores $-100$.
In practice, a preservation metric gives a negation a lower $m$ than its closeness warrants, and MeaningBERT would place the milk negation at $-58.30$.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{What Each Corpus Contributes}

The signed score needs three things from the data: many inference pairs to train the polarity head, a corpus annotated with both relatedness and inference to study how $m$ and $p_{\mathrm{contra}}$ combine, and targeted sets that expose the failures an aggregate score hides.
No single corpus provides all three, so we draw from the five corpora of \autoref{tab:corpora}.

VitaminC \citep{schuster2021vitaminc} supplies the volume, in the regime this paper targets.
Its 488{,}904 pairs are contrastive by construction: a Wikipedia sentence was edited so that the claim supported by its previous revision is refuted by the next, which yields contradictions that differ from their source by a few tokens and not by topic.
SICK \citep{marelli2014sick} links the two quantities, with a relatedness score and an inference label on the same pairs.
MoNLI \citep{geiger2020monli} and NaN-NLI \citep{truong2022nannli} probe negation.
MoNLI has 1{,}202 pairs that differ by one word replaced with a more general or more specific one under a negation that reverses the entailment: ``There is a man not wearing a hat'' entails ``There is a man not wearing a sunhat'', and without the negation the entailment runs the other way.
NaN-NLI has 258 pairs that target negation below the clause level, scoped over a quantifier or a phrase, as in ``Not all people have had the opportunities you have had'' and ``Some people have not had the opportunities you have had''.
Both never enter training and serve as held-out sets (\autoref{tab:final}), since a head that scores well on VitaminC while failing them has learned the corpus and not the relation.
Last, CSMD \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert}, the corpus of human meaning preservation ratings on which MeaningBERT was trained, supplies 407 test pairs to check the signed scale on simplification output.

\subsection{Data Augmentation at the Ends of the Scale}

Inference corpora contain few identical and unrelated pairs, since neither poses an interesting inference problem.
Yet a signed score must be exact on them: $100$ for a sentence paired with itself and $0$ for two sentences with nothing in common.
We therefore train under two conditions, \textsc{raw}, the merged corpus alone, and \textsc{aug}, the same corpus plus 25{,}000 generated pairs of each of three kinds.
An identical pair, $(A, A)$, is labeled entailment, so a sentence paired with itself scores $100$.
An unrelated pair joins two random training sentences that share few tokens and is labeled neutral, so unrelated sentences score $0$ and not a negative value.
A mirrored contradiction swaps an existing contradiction, $(B, A)$ for $(A, B)$, and keeps its label, so a contradiction is detected in either order.

A generated pair receives a label only when that label follows from the original pair.
A contradiction survives the swap, since if $A$ contradicts $B$ then $B$ contradicts $A$.
An entailment does not: ``a dog is running'' entails ``an animal is running'' but not the reverse, so we mirror contradictions only.
Mirroring all of them would double that class, from 50{,}267 to 100{,}534 pairs against 50{,}602 entailments, and push the head to overfit on contradictions.
We therefore mirror a random sample of 25{,}000, the quota of the other two kinds, so each class grows by 25{,}000 and the three stay balanced (\autoref{tab:final}).

\subsection{Stratified Splits}

The development and test splits are stratified by class and by corpus.
Each holds 4{,}000 pairs per class, drawn equally from each corpus, and a corpus too small for its share is taken whole.
Without this, VitaminC, fifty times larger than SICK, would leave SICK at $8.16\%$ of the test split and model selection would rest on Wikipedia claims alone, whereas with it SICK makes up $34.30\%$ of the test split.
Since VitaminC reuses one evidence sentence across many claims, we then remove from the development and training splits every pair whose source sentence appears in a later split.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Encoders.}
We fine-tune the polarity head on seven pretrained encoders (\autoref{tab:main}), all of which read the two sentences jointly, the standard architecture for sentence-pair classification.
They vary along two axes.
The first is the pretraining generation, from BERT \citep{devlin2019bert} to RoBERTa \citep{liu2019roberta} and DeBERTaV3 \citep{he2023debertav3}: \texttt{BERT-base} is the encoder of MeaningBERT, and DeBERTaV3 outperforms the other two on inference benchmarks at equal size \citep{he2023debertav3}.
The second is the task a checkpoint was already fine-tuned on: none, semantic similarity for \texttt{RoBERTa-base\textsubscript{STS-B}}, or inference for \texttt{RoBERTa-large\textsubscript{MNLI}} and the two \texttt{DeBERTa-v3\textsubscript{NLI}} checkpoints.
An inference checkpoint already has a head trained on our three classes and is what a practitioner would use off the shelf (\autoref{an:zeroshot}), so including one tests whether our corpus still adds anything, and pairing each DeBERTaV3 size with its plain checkpoint isolates the effect of inference pretraining.
Checkpoints that have a three-way inference head keep it, with its rows reordered when the published class order differs from ours.

\paragraph{Training.}
Each encoder is trained under \textsc{raw}, with 152{,}060 pairs, and \textsc{aug}, with 227{,}060, over ten random seeds, with the settings and hardware of \autoref{an:training}, and \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large\textsubscript{NLI}} also under the two lexical conditions of \autoref{sec:composition}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We report four measures: macro-F\textsubscript{1} \citep{sokolova2009systematic} on the test split of \autoref{tab:final} for the three-way task, the area under the ROC curve (AUC; \citealp{hanley1982auc}) of $1 - p_{\mathrm{contra}}$ at ranking entailment above contradiction, accuracy on three sanity suites, and accuracy on MoNLI and NaN-NLI.
The AUC measures the quantity the signed score consumes, with $50\%$ as chance, and for a similarity metric it ranks the score directly (\autoref{an:failure}).
The sanity suites hold identical, unrelated and mirrored pairs generated as in \autoref{sec:data} from test-split sentences and check the ends of the scale.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Three baselines without a pretrained encoder bound the polarity head from below.
\texttt{Majority} always predicts the same class.
\texttt{Jaccard} computes the Jaccard overlap \citep{jaccard1912} of the two word sets and predicts neutral below a first threshold, contradiction between the two thresholds and entailment above the second, both fitted on the development split.
\texttt{TF-IDF} trains a logistic regression on word unigrams and bigrams.
For the signed scale, a fourth baseline, the \texttt{Negation lookup}, flips the sign of $m$ when exactly one sentence contains a negation word (\emph{no}, \emph{not}, \emph{n't}, \emph{never}, \emph{nobody}, \emph{none}, \emph{nothing}, \emph{nowhere}, \emph{neither}, \emph{nor} or \emph{without}).

\paragraph{Statistics.}
We compare \textsc{raw} and \textsc{aug} for each encoder with Welch's $t$-test \citep{welch1947} over the ten random seeds, and two encoders with a paired $t$-test matched by random seed.
Each $p$-value comes with Cohen's $d$, since with standard deviations near $0.20$ points a difference of $0.10$ points can be significant yet negligible.
As we compare \textsc{raw} and \textsc{aug} fourteen times, we also apply the Holm-Bonferroni correction \citep{holm1979}.

Polarity does not follow from preservation data.

\section{Composing the Two Heads}
\label{sec:composition}

\subsection{The Composed Score on SICK}

We compose the best-correlated similarity metric of \autoref{tab:cross}, \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large}, with our best polarity head, \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large\textsubscript{NLI}}, by \autoref{eq:compose}, without retraining either.
We measure the result on the SICK half of the test split, 4{,}116 pairs of which 712 are contradictions, and report the mean and standard deviation over the ten heads of \autoref{an:training}, using the \textsc{raw} head as the reference until \autoref{sec:edits} introduces the lexical heads.
The signed score places $83.88 \pm 2.24\%$ of contradictions below zero, against none for the similarity metric alone, keeps $99.87 \pm 0.07\%$ of entailments and $97.23 \pm 0.17\%$ of neutral pairs above zero, and correlates at $r = 0.78 \pm 0.01$ with human relatedness against $0.85$ for the similarity metric alone.
SICK neutrals are related captions, and the unrelated case is tested in \autoref{tab:suites}. \autoref{fig:distribution} shows the signed score per gold class for one head.

\input{figure_distribution}

The $16.12\%$ of contradictions that stay positive are not borderline: their median signed score is $48.85 \pm 1.80$, close to that of neutral pairs, and the head gives them a median $p_{\mathrm{contra}}$ of $0.03 \pm 0.01$.
Only $40.71 \pm 6.83\%$ of them contain an explicit negation, against $99.01 \pm 0.21\%$ of the contradictions placed below zero.
The others oppose antonyms or contrary events, such as ``A little girl is smiling and running outside'' and ``\ldots running inside'', and a few carry debatable labels, where the negated sentence also changes details of the scene.
SICK contradictions are thus mostly explicit negations, and the \texttt{Negation lookup} places $89.75\%$ of them below zero, more than the head, but it also sends $16.15\%$ of neutral pairs below zero, and its correlation with relatedness falls to $0.63$ and $J$ (\autoref{eq:objective}) to $0.56$, against $0.65 \pm 0.02$ for the head.

\subsection{One-Word Edits}
\label{sec:edits}

To separate negation markers from opposition, we edit one word of each SICK test sentence and check the side of zero the signed score falls on (\autoref{tab:minimal}).
An inserted \emph{not} and an antonym (\emph{outside} for \emph{inside}) should send the pair below zero, and a synonym, a hypernym or a co-hyponym (\emph{cat} for \emph{dog}) should keep it above.
The head of \autoref{sec:results}, \textsc{raw}, catches $97.94\%$ of negations but only $11.22\%$ of unseen antonyms, barely more than the lookup, while the off-the-shelf head catches $87.80\%$ of antonyms but sends $74.70\%$ of co-hyponyms below zero.
Our training thus taught negation and lost opposition, which SICK, with only 2{,}150 of the 152{,}060 training pairs, barely exercises.

We therefore train a third condition, \textsc{lex}, which adds to \textsc{raw} (192{,}060 pairs in all) 20{,}000 antonym contradictions and 20{,}000 synonym or hypernym entailments built by one-word substitution in training sentences.
The antonym lexicon is split in two halves, \textsc{lex} sees one, together with antonyms common in Wikipedia claims such as \emph{increased} and \emph{decreased}, and the other half is held out for \autoref{tab:minimal}.
The entailments keep the head from learning that any changed word is a contradiction.
\textsc{lex} places $73.94\%$ of unseen antonyms below zero, against $11.22\%$, keeps synonyms and hypernyms above zero and $94.85\%$ of co-hyponyms, and keeps $91.04 \pm 0.12\%$ macro-F\textsubscript{1}, but its MoNLI accuracy falls from $34.93\%$ to $17.74 \pm 3.19\%$.
On SICK, it places $86.14 \pm 2.20\%$ of contradictions below zero, for a correlation of $0.74 \pm 0.02$ and $J = 0.63 \pm 0.01$.
Adding the lexical pairs to \textsc{aug} instead of \textsc{raw}, \textsc{aug+lex} keeps both gains, with $98.96\%$ on the sanity suites and $76.30\%$ of unseen antonyms below zero, for $90.89 \pm 0.14\%$ macro-F\textsubscript{1} and $19.20\%$ on MoNLI.
On SICK, it places $87.82 \pm 0.85\%$ of contradictions below zero, for $r = 0.72 \pm 0.02$, and we release it as the recommended head.
When antonym and co-hyponym edits accumulate in the same sentence, its score first drops below zero for $29.10\%$ of single edits and then returns above it, to $0.67\%$ after four, as the pair stops describing the same scene.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Factor}

To see what the factor $2$ of \autoref{eq:compose} costs, we replace it with a free factor $\alpha$, clip the result to $[-100, 100]$, and score each value against three requirements multiplied together, so that sacrificing one cannot hide behind the others:
\begin{equation}
J(\alpha) = c(\alpha) \cdot e(\alpha) \cdot \max\!\left(r(\alpha), 0\right),
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
with $c$ the share of contradictions below zero, $e$ the share of entailments above it, and $r$ the Pearson correlation with human relatedness on the pairs whose sign does not flip.

\input{table_composition}

A smaller factor gains little (\autoref{tab:composition}): $\alpha = 1.5$ scores $2.00\%$ higher on $J$ but truncates the negative half at $-50$, so a contradiction can never score as far below zero as its paraphrase scores above it.
A larger factor gains little either, $\alpha = 3$ placing $1.24$ more points of contradictions below zero for $0.06$ of correlation.
With one head, on the development split, and MeaningBERT in place of \texttt{DeBERTa-v3-large}, $J$ again peaks at $\alpha = 1.5$ and, among the factors that reach $-100$, at $\alpha = 2$.
Since the sign depends on $p_{\mathrm{contra}}$ alone, only $r$ depends on the similarity metric.

\subsection{What Fine-Tuning the Polarity Head Buys}

With the off-the-shelf head (\autoref{an:zeroshot}), $97.61\%$ of contradictions fall below zero against $83.88\%$, but the correlation with relatedness drops by $0.18$ and $J$ by $12.05\%$ relative.
\autoref{fig:reliability} shows why: among the pairs it gives a contradiction probability near $0.5$, fewer than one in ten is a contradiction, so the product lowers the score of merely unrelated pairs.

\subsection{What the Sign Buys at the Decision Point}

\autoref{tab:decision} gives, at three acceptance thresholds, the share of accepted pairs that are contradictions and the share of entailments kept.
At a threshold of $50$, $14.10\%$ of the pairs the similarity metric accepts are contradictions, against $2.03 \pm 0.09\%$ for \textsc{raw}, $1.42 \pm 0.18\%$ for \textsc{lex} and $1.26 \pm 0.11\%$ for \textsc{aug+lex}, a sevenfold to elevenfold reduction with non-overlapping bootstrap intervals, and $2.30\%$ for the lookup.
Entailment recall falls only from $99.15\%$ to between $98.17\%$ and $98.31\%$, so the signed scale does not simply lower every score.
Across thresholds from $0$ to $95$ in steps of five, \textsc{raw} never accepts more contradictions than the similarity metric and never loses more than $3.85$ points of recall on average.
The off-the-shelf head accepts $0.15\%$ of contradictions for $96.72\%$ of entailments, so the gain comes from the shape of the scale and fine-tuning buys recall.
On simplification output, where contradictions are rare, the sign selects the pairs humans rated worst (\autoref{an:simplification}).

\section{Conclusion}

Meaning preservation metrics output a non-negative score, so they cannot separate a contradiction from a pair that shares little meaning, and a published metric ranks entailment above contradiction no better than word overlap.
We proposed a signed scale on $[-100, 100]$ that keeps any similarity metric as it is and multiplies its score by $1 - 2\,p_{\mathrm{contra}}$, so the closeness of a pair sets the absolute value and a polarity head sets the sign.
The polarity head is learnable on a stratified, leakage-controlled inference corpus, reaching $91.10\%$ macro-F\textsubscript{1}, and generated identical, unrelated and mirrored pairs make it reliable at both ends of the scale.
Composed with an existing metric, the signed score places $83.88\%$ of SICK contradictions below zero, where the similarity metric alone places none, and accepts seven times fewer contradictions at a fixed threshold while keeping almost every entailment.
One-word edits show what a word list cannot do: a head trained with generated antonym contradictions places $73.94\%$ of unseen antonyms below zero, against $11.22\%$ without them and none for a negation lookup.
Combined with the generated identical and unrelated pairs, it keeps the ends of the scale and cuts accepted contradictions elevenfold, at a cost of $0.21$ points of macro-F\textsubscript{1} and of correlation with relatedness.

\section*{Limitations}

Seven limitations bound our claims.

First, there is no signed gold standard.
The composition is evaluated against three necessary conditions rather than against human signed judgments, because none exist.
A corpus of human-rated signed pairs would let the form of \autoref{eq:compose} be tested rather than argued, and collecting one is the natural next step.

Second, data augmentation costs monotonicity.
The gain in sanity is paid for on MoNLI, which drops on three of seven encoders and on both large DeBERTa variants, by $-6.82$ points on the plain one and $-3.69$ on the inference-pretrained one.
Generated identical and unrelated pairs teach the easy ends of the scale, not the reversal of entailment under negation, and the lexical conditions halve MoNLI accuracy again, from $34.93\%$ to $17.74\%$ and $19.20\%$.

Third, a single corpus carries the composition.
Every measurement of the signed scale lives on the SICK half of the test split, 4{,}116 pairs of which 712 are contradictions, which is also where the sensitivity to the factor is measured.
The constraint is structural rather than a choice: SICK is the only corpus we know of annotating relatedness and inference over the same pairs, and the third requirement of \autoref{eq:objective} needs both.
It binds the sensitivity analysis unavoidably and the evaluation only by convenience, since \autoref{tab:decision} needs inference labels alone and could in principle be measured elsewhere.
The split is also thin on the development side: SICK supplies 495 development rows, of which 229 shared source sentences with its own test portion and were removed, leaving the development split at $2.27\%$ SICK against $34.30\%$ in test.
Model selection is therefore driven by VitaminC while the composition is analyzed on SICK alone, and the two numbers are the first thing to watch should they diverge.

Fourth, the sign costs correlation.
On both human-rated quantities the composition correlates worse than the magnitude alone: $0.78$ against $0.85$ on SICK relatedness, and $0.64$ against $0.70$ on preservation ratings.
This is what should happen, since neither rating scale is signed and a correct negative score can only move a pair away from a non-negative target.
Correlation with a non-negative rating is therefore the wrong instrument here, and \autoref{tab:decision} measures the benefit where it exists instead.
What no existing human judgement can settle is whether the magnitude of a negative score is right, which a signed annotation would.
That magnitude also depends on where $m$ comes from: a preservation metric already lowers $m$ for the negation it partly detects, so with MeaningBERT the negation of \autoref{fig:scale} would land at $-58.30$ rather than $-100$, whereas a relatedness metric would not count the negation twice.

Fifth, the sign rests on one head.
Because the composition is a product with $m \geq 0$, the sign of the score is decided entirely by $p_{\mathrm{contra}}$, and a related pair on which the head is undecided, at $p_{\mathrm{contra}} = 0.5$, scores $0$ like an unrelated one.
A polarity head that is wrong about a pair is not corrected by a good similarity metric, and the $16.12\%$ of SICK contradictions that stay positive are exactly the pairs the head misses, mostly contradictions without an explicit negation (\autoref{sec:composition}).
The one-word edits that measure this rest on hand-written lexicons for SICK's vocabulary, and a few antonym swaps, such as \emph{large} and \emph{small} gathering, are contrary only under the same-scene convention.
The lexical conditions learn antonyms from that same convention, and $23.70\%$ to $26.06\%$ of unseen antonyms still stay positive.
We also designed them after seeing the \textsc{raw} head fail on these edits, so the held-out half of the lexicon guards against memorized word pairs but not against a test built with the remedy in mind.

Sixth, the property the title names is never measured on natural unrelated pairs.
That two sentences about different topics score near zero follows from the product form, since $m \approx 0$ leaves the polarity term nothing to act on, and it is measured on the generated unrelated suite.
No corpus we know of annotates genuinely unrelated pairs for meaning preservation, because such a pair is not an interesting annotation, so the construction carries this half of the claim and the evidence for it is self-generated.

Finally, the work is English and sentence-level throughout.
Every corpus we merge is English, and the scale is defined over sentence pairs.
Neither restriction is incidental.
The polarity half rests on inference corpora, which are abundant in English and thin in most other languages, so the composition cannot be ported by translating a metric alone.
Contradiction between documents is also a different relation from contradiction between sentences, since a document can deny another in one passage and agree with it everywhere else, and a single scalar cannot carry that.
Whether the product form survives either move is untested.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
A meaning preservation metric is deployed to decide whether a rewritten sentence may be shown to a reader in place of the original.
When such a metric reports that a negated sentence preserves the meaning of its source, as we measure in \autoref{sec:results}, the error reaches the reader rather than the engineer.
The risk is concrete in the settings where simplification is most useful, where a layperson may rely on the rewritten text without access to the original \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert}.
A signed scale does not remove that risk, but it makes the failure visible to the system consuming the score instead of hiding it inside a positive number.
Our corpora also inherit the biases of the Wikipedia revisions and caption corpora they are drawn from, and a polarity head trained on them carries those biases into whatever it gates \citep{bender2021dangers}.

\section{Corpus Construction and Leakage Control}
\label{an:corpus}

\subsection{Merging and Harmonization}

The two training corpora, VitaminC and SICK, are merged into one table with a unified three-way polarity column. MoNLI and NaN-NLI are kept apart and never enter it.
VitaminC carries \textsc{fact3} labels, which we map onto \textsc{nli3} as \textsc{supports} to entailment, \textsc{not enough info} to neutral and \textsc{refutes} to contradiction.
SICK is assembled from two public halves, one carrying the inference label and one the relatedness score. The join is verified to lose no more than 1\% of rows, and in practice loses none.

\subsection{Stratification}

We keep the train, development and test splits that each corpus publishes. The development and test splits are then reduced to 4{,}000 rows per class, drawn in equal share from each corpus by water-filling: a corpus smaller than its share is taken whole and the shortfall is redistributed over the larger ones.
Without it, SICK falls to 8.16\% of the test split and 0.78\% of the development split.

\subsection{Leakage}

VitaminC reuses one piece of evidence across many claims, and its published splits do not separate evidence sentences, so 1{,}662 source sentences, 5.6\% of the test groups, were also present in training.
Once the stratified test split is fixed, we remove the rows that share a source sentence with a later split: 229 development rows whose source sentence appears in test, then 2{,}379 training rows whose source sentence appears in development or test.
Rows are removed from development and training rather than from test so that the test split stays fixed and balanced. Development only selects models and can afford the loss.
We then assert that train/dev, train/test and dev/test share no pair and no source sentence, and run the assertion again after data augmentation, since a generated pair inherits its source sentences.

\subsection{Data Augmentation Quotas}

Each generated kind of \autoref{sec:data} feeds one class: identical pairs feed entailment, unrelated pairs feed neutral and mirrored contradictions feed contradiction.
Each contributes exactly 25{,}000 pairs, so every class grows by the same amount and the training split stays balanced, going from 152{,}060 to 227{,}060 rows.
The quota is needed because the three kinds are not naturally equal in number: mirroring every contradiction would add 50{,}267 pairs to that class alone, doubling it to 100{,}534 against 50{,}602 entailments.
To reach the quota, each generator is asked for 40\% more pairs than needed, since the unrelated generator rejects draws whose sentences overlap too much and the identical one skips sentences already used, and the surplus is discarded at random.

\section{Measuring Polarity in a Similarity Metric}
\label{an:failure}

The similarity-metric measurements of \autoref{sec:results} train nothing and are deterministic, so they involve no random seed.

\subsection{Model}

We score MeaningBERT \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert} with its public checkpoint, without fine-tuning or recalibration.
For each pair, it returns one meaning preservation score on $[0, 100]$, and that score is the only quantity used.

\subsection{Data}

The main measurement uses the test split of \autoref{tab:final}: 12{,}000 pairs, 4{,}000 per class, drawn from SICK and VitaminC by the stratification of \autoref{an:corpus}.
The AUC uses its 4{,}000 entailment and 4{,}000 contradiction pairs. Neutral pairs are excluded, since a similarity metric is expected to score them low and they say nothing about polarity.
The SICK figure uses a separate sample: the first 600 entailment and the first 600 contradiction pairs of the public SICK test set, in their published order.
On this sample the checkpoint scores entailments at $67.34$ on average and contradictions at $69.18$.

\subsection{Statistic}

The AUC is the probability that a randomly drawn entailment pair receives a higher score than a randomly drawn contradiction pair, counting a tie as one half.
We compute it from mid-ranks through its equivalence with the Mann-Whitney statistic \citep{mann1947whitney,hanley1982auc}.
Ties matter here because a clamped output head saturates at exactly $0$ and $100$, and ordinal ranks would break those ties by position in the array.
An AUC of $50\%$ means the score does not order the two classes, and an AUC below $50\%$ means it orders them backwards.

\subsection{Surface Baseline}

Both sentences are lowercased and split into alphanumeric tokens, and the score of a pair is the Jaccard overlap of the two token sets.
This score enters the AUC directly and involves no training.
The two thresholds of \autoref{sec:results}, fitted on the development split, serve only to turn the same score into three classes for macro-F\textsubscript{1}.

\subsection{Head Reuse}

Four of the seven checkpoints ship a three-way inference head.
We keep it when its class order matches ours and permute its rows when the published order is reversed, as it is for \texttt{roberta-large-mnli}.
The alternative, training from a permuted head, is silent and costs an epoch of relearning the permutation.

\subsection{Random Seeds}

Random seeds $[42, \ldots, 51]$ control initialization of the new head, data order and dropout.
Unless stated otherwise, every number reported in this paper is a mean over the ten with its standard deviation.
The AUC column of \autoref{tab:cross} and the per-corpus figures of \autoref{sec:results} come from ten further runs of the best cell that keep their weights, since the grid stores predicted classes and an AUC needs probabilities.
Nine of those ten use a micro-batch of $8$ with accumulation $4$ on a later card, the effective batch unchanged at $32$.
\autoref{sec:composition} composes the same ten heads. \autoref{fig:distribution}, \autoref{fig:reliability}, the comparison with MeaningBERT and the check on simplification output use the head of random seed $42$ alone.

\subsection{Hardware}

The grid runs on three NVIDIA Pascal-generation cards: a GTX~1080~Ti (11\,GB), a Quadro~P5000 (16\,GB) and a Titan~Xp (12\,GB).
All training is fp32, since the generation has no usable bfloat16.
The ten \textsc{lex} heads of \autoref{sec:composition} and the ten \textsc{aug+lex} heads were trained later on three NVIDIA RTX~6000 Ada cards, in fp32 with a micro-batch of $16$ and accumulation $2$, the effective batch unchanged at $32$, each run taking about 85 and 115 minutes.

\subsection{Budget}

The grid takes roughly 500 GPU-hours in total.
A base-encoder run takes 68 minutes on the \textsc{raw} condition and 109 minutes on \textsc{aug}. A large-encoder run takes 318 and 489 minutes.
The composition of \autoref{sec:composition} trains nothing: it is two forward passes over 4{,}116 pairs plus the sensitivity sweep of \autoref{sec:composition}, and runs on CPU in under half an hour.
No private API was used, and the total monetary cost is the electricity of hardware we already own.
